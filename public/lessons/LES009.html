1🏗️ Why setup determines everything
🎯 The first week of a client relationship sets the tone for everything that follows. Get it right and the relationship runs itself.
The Foundation Rule

A client relationship is like a building — what you see above ground is only as strong as what was built below. A VA who skips setup steps works in constant chaos: unclear deliverables, missed deadlines, scope disagreements, and clients who feel like they are always chasing updates. A VA who sets up correctly works in clarity — because every expectation was agreed upon before the work began.

Most VAs lose clients not because their work is bad, but because the expectations were never defined. The client assumed daily updates. The VA assumed weekly. The client expected unlimited revisions. The VA expected one round. The client thought the rate covered three platforms. The VA quoted for one. None of these are skill failures — they are setup failures. And every single one is preventable with a proper onboarding process before the first deliverable is produced.

Professional setup communicates something even more important than the work itself: it tells the client they hired someone who runs a real business. It signals reliability, organization, and accountability before a single task is completed. Clients who experience a structured onboarding almost never leave in the first 90 days — because the relationship started with clarity instead of assumption.

2📞 The discovery call — questions and what to gather

The discovery call is not a sales pitch — it is an information-gathering session. Your goal is to understand the client's business, their needs, their working style, and their expectations well enough to write an accurate service agreement and plan the first month of work. A 20 to 30 minute call done correctly eliminates the three most common sources of client conflict: scope disagreements, communication misalignment, and unclear deadlines.

How to structure a 25-minute discovery call:

  • 1
    Minutes 0-5 — Rapport and context. "Tell me a bit about your business and what you are working on right now." Let them talk. Do not interrupt. Listen for the pain points they mention voluntarily — these are the ones that matter most to them.
  • 2
    Minutes 5-15 — Your structured questions. Work through the essential questions below. Take notes. Do not rely on memory — you will miss details that matter for the contract.
  • 3
    Minutes 15-22 — Clarify scope. Based on what they said, describe back to them what you heard: "So what I am hearing is that you need X, Y, and Z — is that right?" This surfaces assumptions before they become problems.
  • 4
    Minutes 22-25 — Next steps. Tell them what happens next: "I will send you a service agreement by tomorrow. Once that is signed I will send the welcome email with everything I need to get started."
📋 DISCOVERY CALL QUESTION LIST — USE THIS ON EVERY CALL
About Their Business
— What does your business do and who is your ideal customer?
— How long have you been running this business and do you have a team?
— What platforms or tools are you currently using? (Instagram, Facebook, Buffer, Canva, etc.)
— What has your social media / content / admin situation looked like for the past 3 months?
About the Role
— What specific tasks do you need handled every week?
— What does success look like for you in 3 months?
— Have you worked with a VA before? If yes, what worked and what did not?
— Are there tasks that are off-limits or that you always want to handle yourself?
About Working Style
— Do you prefer async communication (Slack, email) or regular calls?
— How often do you want updates — daily, weekly, bi-weekly?
— What is your preferred response time expectation for messages?
— Are you comfortable with me working independently once briefed, or do you prefer step-by-step approvals?
About Logistics
— What is your budget range for this role?
— Are you looking for a trial period first or a monthly retainer from the start?
— What is your preferred payment method and schedule? (Weekly, bi-monthly, monthly?)
— When would you like to start and what is the most urgent thing in the first week?
💡
Record Your Calls (With Permission)

Always ask: "Do you mind if I record this so I can refer back to my notes?" Almost every client says yes. A recording means you never miss a detail that ends up in a contract disagreement later. Use Loom, Zoom, or Google Meet recording. Store it in the client folder. Review it before writing the service agreement.

⚠️
Red Flags During the Discovery Call

Watch for: (1) "I do not want a contract — let us just start." This means no accountability — always require a written agreement. (2) "My budget is flexible" — always nail down a number before writing a contract. (3) "The last VA could not keep up with me" — dig deeper before committing. (4) "I need someone available on weekends" — decide immediately if that fits your boundaries. (5) Reluctance to answer questions about budget or scope — a client who avoids clarity now will create conflict later.

3📄 Writing a clear service agreement
Definition

A service agreement is a written document that defines exactly what you will deliver, how much the client will pay, when payment is due, what happens if things change, and how either party can end the relationship. It protects the client from an unreliable VA and protects you from a client who changes the scope without paying more.

Many new VAs skip the contract because they fear it will scare the client away. The opposite is true. A professional contract signals that you run a real business — and real business owners feel safer working with someone who operates with proper documentation. A client who refuses a reasonable contract is not a client you want: they are avoiding accountability.

  • 1
    Clause 1 — Scope of Services
    Exactly what you will and will not do
    List every deliverable by name and quantity. "5 Instagram feed posts per week, designed in Canva using the client's brand kit, scheduled via Buffer by Sunday 11:59 PM." Do not write "social media management" — that phrase means different things to different people and is the most common source of scope disagreements. Be specific to the point of tedium. Every task that is NOT listed here is out of scope.
  • 2
    Clause 2 — Rate and Payment Terms
    How much, when, and how
    State the monthly retainer amount or hourly rate. State the payment due date (e.g. "Payment due on the 1st of each month for services rendered in that month"). State the payment method (GCash, PayPal, bank transfer). State what happens if payment is late: "A 5% late fee applies to invoices unpaid after 5 business days." This clause prevents the most stressful scenario in freelancing: chasing overdue payments with no written leverage.
  • 3
    Clause 3 — Revision Policy
    How many revisions are included and what counts as one
    Define this precisely: "Two rounds of revisions are included per deliverable. A revision is defined as minor changes to an existing design — changes to the concept, layout, or brief after approval constitute a new deliverable and will be invoiced separately." Without this clause, a client can request unlimited revisions and you have no grounds to push back.
  • 4
    Clause 4 — Confidentiality
    You will not share their business information
    A simple clause: "The VA agrees to keep all client information, business data, passwords, strategies, and materials strictly confidential and will not disclose them to any third party without written consent." This reassures clients who are sharing access to their accounts, inboxes, and brand assets — which is everything.
  • 5
    Clause 5 — Termination Policy
    How either party can end the relationship
    "Either party may terminate this agreement with 14 days written notice. Any work completed within the notice period will be invoiced and due at the end of the notice period." This protects you from being dropped with no notice and protects the client from being abandoned mid-project. Fourteen days gives both parties time to transition without chaos.
💡
How to Send the Contract

Send via Google Docs with comment permissions enabled — the client can leave questions inline. Or use HelloSign or DocuSign for electronic signatures (free tiers available). Never send a PDF that requires printing, signing, scanning, and emailing back. That friction causes delays. A Google Doc with a request for written confirmation ("Reply to confirm you agree to these terms") is legally sufficient in most freelance contexts — consult local regulations for large contracts.

4📁 Setting up your client onboarding system

Onboarding is the bridge between a signed contract and the first deliverable. It is the moment the client sees what kind of professional they hired. A great onboarding experience makes the client feel that they made the right decision. A chaotic one — missing files, repeated requests, unclear instructions — plants the first seed of doubt that grows into a cancelled contract.

The 4 components of a complete client onboarding system:

  • 📧
    Welcome Email. Sent within 24 hours of contract signing. Sets the tone, confirms the start date, lists what you need from them, and gives them the link to the shared workspace. See the full template below.
  • 📁
    Shared Google Drive Folder. Create a clearly named folder: "CLIENT NAME — [Your Name] VA." Inside: a Brand Assets subfolder, a Content subfolder, a Reports subfolder, and a Contracts subfolder. Share with the client and confirm they have access before Day 1.
  • 🎨
    Brand Asset Collection. Request: logo files (PNG with transparent background), brand color hex codes, brand fonts, any existing templates, and a Google Doc with their brand voice guidelines if they have one. Without these you cannot produce on-brand work — and working without them is one of the most common causes of revision requests.
  • 🔑
    Access Requests. Request credentials or admin access to every platform you will manage: Instagram Business Manager, Facebook Page, Buffer, Canva (add you as a team member), their website admin if relevant. Use a secure password manager like LastPass or 1Password shared folder — never store passwords in a plain text document.

5🗂️ Creating a project workspace

A project workspace is where the client can always see what is being worked on, what is complete, and what is coming up next. It removes the most anxiety-producing question in any client relationship: "What is my VA actually doing right now?" When a client can open a Trello board at any time and see every task in progress, they stop worrying — and they stop sending "just checking in" messages that interrupt your workflow.

❌ No Workspace — Client Anxiety
  • "Is she even working on this?"
  • Three check-in messages in one week
  • Client feels out of the loop and nervous
  • VA has to stop work to reassure client
  • Scope creep starts because client fills the void
  • Trust erodes without visibility
✅ Clear Workspace — Client Confidence
  • Client checks the board and sees progress
  • Zero unnecessary messages during work hours
  • Client feels informed and in control
  • VA works without interruption
  • Scope is clear because the board defines it
  • Trust compounds with every completed card

How to set up a Trello board for a social media VA client:

  • 📋
    List 1 — Content Calendar. One card per week showing the planned posts. Each card contains: post dates, captions (draft), graphic file links, and scheduling status. The client can comment on cards with feedback.
  • 🔄
    List 2 — In Progress. Tasks currently being worked on. One card per active deliverable. Move cards here when you start working on them so the client always knows what is active.
  • 👀
    List 3 — Needs Client Review. Completed items waiting for client approval. This is the client's action queue — they know they need to look at anything that lands here. Set a 48-hour review window expectation.
  • ✅
    List 4 — Done. Approved and published content. Archive monthly to keep the board clean. This column is also your evidence of work delivered — screenshot it for your monthly report.
  • ⚠️
    List 5 — Blocked / Needs Info. Anything you cannot complete without client input. This forces issues to the surface immediately instead of silently missing a deadline.
💡
Notion vs Trello vs Asana — Which to Use?

Use Trello for most clients — it is visual, intuitive, and has a generous free tier. Use Notion for clients who prefer a document-style workspace or who already use Notion for their business. Use Asana for more complex projects with multiple deliverables and team members. The best tool is the one the client will actually use — always ask during the discovery call which tools they are already comfortable with.

6💬 Establishing communication expectations

Communication misalignment is the silent relationship killer in VA work. The client expects a reply in 30 minutes. You work from 9am to 5pm. They message at 9pm on a Friday. You do not reply until Monday. They feel ignored. You feel harassed. The relationship sours — and no amount of good work will repair the resentment of a boundary violation that was never even discussed. This entire problem is preventable with one document sent on Day 1.

Your Communication Expectations Document should cover 5 things:

  • ⏰
    Working Hours. State your exact hours and time zone. "I work Monday to Friday, 9am to 6pm Philippine Time. Messages received outside these hours will be replied to on the next working day." This is not negotiable — it is your boundary. State it professionally and firmly from Day 1.
  • 💬
    Preferred Channel. Pick one primary channel and stick to it. "All project communication goes through Slack. Email is for contracts and invoices only. Messages sent to my personal Facebook will not be monitored for work updates." Multiple channels create chaos and things fall through the cracks.
  • ⚡
    Response Time Commitment. What the client can expect from you: "I respond to all Slack messages within 4 business hours during working hours." What you need from them: "For content approvals, I need feedback within 48 hours to maintain the publishing schedule. If approval is not received within 48 hours, content will be scheduled as submitted."
  • 📅
    Update Schedule. When and how you will proactively update the client without them asking. "Every Monday morning I send a brief Slack update covering what was completed last week, what is in progress this week, and anything I need from you." This one habit alone eliminates 80% of client anxiety messages.
  • 🚨
    Urgent Issue Protocol. Define what counts as urgent and how you handle it. "If something requires immediate attention outside working hours — a published error, an account issue — please email me with URGENT in the subject line and I will respond within 2 hours." This gives clients a safety valve while protecting your off-hours time.
✅ Sample Communication Expectations Statement

"My working hours are Monday to Friday, 9am to 6pm Philippine Time. All project communication happens via Slack — I respond within 4 business hours. I send a Monday morning update every week covering last week and the week ahead. For content reviews, please send feedback within 48 hours so the schedule stays on track. For urgent issues outside working hours, email with URGENT in the subject line."

7🚀 The first 30 days with a new client

The first 30 days set the tone for the entire relationship. Do them right and you earn a client who trusts you completely, stops micromanaging, and starts referring you. Do them poorly and you spend months trying to recover from a first impression that never fully heals. The goal in Month 1 is not to impress with volume — it is to impress with reliability, communication, and clarity.

  • W1
    Week 1 — Foundation
    Set up everything before producing anything
    Complete all onboarding steps: welcome email sent, Drive folder set up, brand assets collected, access credentials received, Trello board live. Send your first Monday update even if it only says "All systems are set up and I am beginning the first content batch today." Delivering structure in Week 1 tells the client they made a great hire before a single piece of work is submitted.
  • W2
    Week 2 — First Deliverables
    Deliver early, ask for feedback, iterate fast
    Submit the first batch of work 1 to 2 days early. This creates instant goodwill and signals that you work ahead of schedule, not behind. When submitting, include a brief note: "Here is your first content batch. I have tried to match the tone from your existing posts and used the brand colors from your guide. Please let me know if anything needs adjusting — I want to lock in the style before producing next week's batch."
  • W3
    Week 3 — Style Lock-In
    Apply all feedback and establish your rhythm
    Incorporate every piece of feedback from Week 2 and confirm: "I have updated the style based on your notes — does this feel right?" Once the client approves, your production workflow is locked. You no longer need to ask style questions for routine content. Note any patterns in their feedback (they always prefer a certain tone, always want certain colors) and build those into your template system.
  • W4
    Week 4 — First Review and Next Month Plan
    Send a mini report and propose Month 2
    At the end of Week 4, send a brief summary: what was posted, any early analytics wins, and one or two ideas you have for improving Month 2. This is not your full monthly report — it is a preview that shows strategic thinking. It also creates a natural moment to confirm the next month's scope and payment before the month starts. Clients who receive a Week 4 review almost never drop VAs before Month 2 begins.
✅
The 30-Day Trust Formula

Deliver on time every time in Month 1. Communicate proactively before the client has to ask. Show improvement after every round of feedback. Send the Monday update without being reminded. Do these four things consistently for 30 days and you will have a client who renews automatically, raises no objections to rate increases, and introduces you to other business owners. Reliability is the rarest and most valuable trait a VA can demonstrate in the first month.

8📅 Setting milestones and deliverable deadlines

A milestone is a scheduled checkpoint in your project — a date by which a specific deliverable must be complete. Without milestones, projects drift. Weeks pass, work accumulates, and suddenly you have a client asking "where is the content calendar for this month?" and you are scrambling. With milestones, every week has a target, and both you and the client always know exactly where you are in the project cycle.

A standard monthly milestone framework for a social media VA:

  • 📅
    1st of the month — Content calendar submitted for approval. The full month's content plan in a Google Sheet: every post date, content type, topic, and caption draft. Client has until the 3rd to approve.
  • 📅
    3rd of the month — Client approval received. Once approved, you begin producing graphics and finalizing captions. If approval is late, the publishing schedule shifts — document this in writing.
  • 📅
    7th of the month — Week 1 content batch complete and scheduled. All Week 1 posts live in Buffer and ready to go. Client can review in the Trello board.
  • 📅
    14th of the month — Week 2 batch complete, Week 3 drafts submitted. Content is always two weeks ahead to allow for revisions without schedule disruption.
  • 📅
    25th of the month — Data pull for monthly report. Screenshot all analytics before the month closes. Begin drafting the report.
  • 📅
    1st of next month — Monthly report sent. Full report with key metrics, top posts, challenges, and next month recommendations.
💡
Put Milestones in the Contract

Include your milestone schedule as an attachment to the service agreement. When a client signs, they are also agreeing to your delivery timeline — including their responsibilities within it (feedback windows, approval deadlines). This prevents the common situation where a client delays approval for 2 weeks and then blames the VA for a publishing gap.

⚠️
Missed Milestones — What to Do

If you miss a milestone: communicate immediately, explain the cause factually, state the new target date, and take accountability without over-apologizing. "I am running 2 days behind on the Week 3 batch because the client feedback on Week 2 came in late. New delivery is Thursday." One factual sentence is more professional than five apologetic ones. If the client caused the delay — their late approval shifted the timeline — state that clearly and calmly in writing.

9🚧 How to handle scope creep professionally
Definition

Scope creep is the gradual expansion of your deliverables beyond what was agreed in the contract — without a corresponding increase in pay. It starts small: "Can you also reply to our comments?" Then: "Can you draft the email newsletter this week?" Then: "Can you manage our TikTok too?" Each request seems reasonable in isolation. Together they double your workload for the same rate.

Scope creep is almost never malicious. Most clients genuinely do not realize they are adding work — they just see things that need doing and assume you will help. Your job is to catch scope creep early, name it clearly, and address it professionally before it builds resentment on your side and confusion on theirs. The earlier you address it, the easier it is. Left unaddressed, it becomes the primary reason long-term VAs burn out and quit good clients.

The 3-step scope creep response framework:

  • 1
    Acknowledge the request warmly. Never refuse flatly. "Happy to help with that!" signals goodwill and prevents defensiveness.
  • 2
    Name the scope boundary clearly. "That is not currently in our agreement — I handle [what you agreed], but [new request] is outside that scope." Reference the contract, not your personal preference. This keeps the conversation factual.
  • 3
    Offer a path forward. "I can add that to our arrangement from next month for an additional [X] per month, or I can do it as a one-time task for [X]. Which would work better for you?" Give them a choice, not a dead end.
✅ Sample Scope Creep Response

"Happy to help with the email newsletter! That is not currently in our agreement — I am handling Instagram and Facebook content only. I can add newsletter drafting to our monthly scope for an additional 3,000 pesos per month starting August, or do this one-off for 800 pesos. Would either of those work for you?"

⚠️
The Two Worst Responses to Scope Creep

(1) Saying yes and doing it for free — this trains the client to keep asking, erodes the value of your agreement, and sets a precedent that your scope is negotiable without discussion. (2) Refusing harshly without offering an alternative — this makes the client feel penalized for a reasonable business request and damages the relationship unnecessarily. The correct middle is: acknowledge warmly, name the boundary, offer a paid path forward.

10🏁 Knowing when and how to close a project

Client projects end in one of three ways: natural completion, contract non-renewal, or early termination. Each requires a different process — but all three share the same foundation: handle the ending as professionally as you handled the beginning. How you close a project determines whether you get a testimonial, a referral, or a negative reputation. Every ending is a professional moment.

💡
The 14-Day Notice Is Your Friend

Your termination clause gives both parties 14 days. Use this time wisely — do not coast. Deliver everything in progress to a high standard. A client who receives excellent work during a notice period often changes their mind, or — if they do not — gives you an excellent testimonial and refers you. The notice period is your final impression. Make it count.

11📦 How to offboard a client professionally

Offboarding is the reverse of onboarding — and it deserves the same care. A professional offboard ensures the client has everything they need to continue without you, that no loose ends remain, and that your reputation stays intact. A VA who disappears at the end of a contract — leaving the client scrambling to find passwords, missing files, and unfinished work — will never get a testimonial or referral from that client. A VA who offboards cleanly almost always does.

The complete offboarding checklist — use this for every project ending:

  • 📁
    Final Deliverables Package. Compile all work product into the client's Drive folder: final content files, completed graphics, any documents you created. Organize clearly by month and type. The client should be able to find anything within 30 seconds.
  • 📊
    Final Monthly Report. Even if the relationship is ending, deliver the final report. This is a professional obligation — and it is your final proof of value that may prompt a referral.
  • 🔑
    Access Removal. Remove yourself from every platform you were added to: Instagram Business Manager, Facebook Page, Buffer, Canva, their website. Send a message confirming: "I have removed my access from all platforms as of [date]. Please confirm everything looks correct on your end."
  • 📋
    Handover Notes Document. Create a Google Doc with: your working process for each task (so a new VA can pick up where you left off), any recurring deadlines the client should be aware of, platform login notes (without passwords — just confirmation that credentials exist in their password manager), and any ongoing campaigns or commitments that need attention.
  • 💌
    Professional Goodbye Message. A warm, brief message thanking the client, wishing them well, and asking for a testimonial. See the sample below.
  • 🧾
    Final Invoice. Send within 24 hours of the final work day. Include all work completed in the final period and confirm payment method and due date.

Hi [Name],

Today is my last working day on your account and I want to make sure the handover is completely smooth for you.

Everything is organized in your Drive folder under [FOLDER LINK]. I have also left a Handover Notes document with my working process for each task, recurring deadlines, and anything else a new VA would need to pick up where I left off.

I have removed my access from Instagram, Facebook, Buffer, and Canva as of today. Please confirm on your end that everything looks correct.

Your final invoice for [PERIOD] is attached — payment details are included.

Wishing you all the best,

12⭐ Requesting testimonials and referrals

A testimonial is more valuable than any certificate or portfolio piece. It is social proof from a real person who paid real money and got real results. A referral is even more powerful — it is a warm introduction from someone your prospect already trusts. Both are available to you at the end of every successful client relationship. Most VAs never ask — and so they never receive them. The ask is simple. The timing matters. And the outcome compounds.

When to ask for a testimonial — the 3 best moments:

  • 🏆
    After a specific win. "Your reach hit a record high this month and you gained 80 new followers — I would love to ask if you would be willing to write a short testimonial about our work together." Timing the ask to a visible win produces the most enthusiastic responses.
  • 📅
    At the 3-month mark. Three months is enough time to show real results and enough time for the client to have formed a clear opinion of your work. Ask in the Week 4 summary of Month 3: "We have been working together for 3 months now — I would love to ask for a quick testimonial if you have been happy with the work."
  • 💌
    In the goodbye message. The offboarding message is the natural moment for a testimonial request — the relationship is at its most complete and the client is reflecting on the whole experience. See the goodbye template in Section 11.

How to make the ask easy to say yes to:

  • 📝
    Give them a prompt, not a blank page. "Would you be willing to write 2-3 sentences about your experience — specifically what you liked about how I work and any results you noticed?" A specific prompt is 5x more likely to get a response than "Can you write me a testimonial?"
  • 🔗
    Make it one click. Send a Google Form link with 3 short questions. Remove all friction. A client who has to think about where to post, what format to use, and how long to write will not do it — not because they do not want to, but because life gets in the way.
  • 🎁
    Offer to draft it for them. "If it is easier, I can draft a few sentences based on our work together and you can edit it however you like — just let me know." Many clients prefer this. Your draft becomes their words with minor tweaks. This is not dishonest — it is practical.
✅ Sample Testimonial Request Message

"Hi [Name] — working with you these past 4 months has been genuinely rewarding and I am so proud of what we built together. I have one small ask: would you be willing to write 2-3 sentences about your experience working with me — specifically what you liked about how I manage the work and any results you noticed? I have a quick Google Form here if that is easiest: [LINK]. Alternatively, I am happy to draft something for you to edit. Either way — thank you so much for a great project."

💡
Asking for Referrals

After receiving a testimonial, follow up: "Thank you so much — this means a lot. One more thing: do you know any other business owners who could use a social media VA? I have one opening next month and would love a warm introduction if you think of anyone." Referrals from happy clients convert at 10x the rate of cold outreach. One referral ask after a successful project is worth 50 cold applications.

🎯 Lesson 9 — Key Takeaways

  • 1
    74% of early client terminations are caused by unclear expectations — setup failures, not skill failures. Always set up before you start.
  • 2
    The discovery call is an information-gathering session, not a sales pitch. Run it with structured questions and take notes on everything.
  • 3
    A service agreement with 5 core clauses — scope, payment, revisions, confidentiality, and termination — protects both parties and sets professional standards from Day 1.
  • 4
    Send the welcome email within 24 hours of contract signing. Set up the Drive folder, collect brand assets, and request all platform access before producing a single deliverable.
  • 5
    A Trello board with 5 lists — Content Calendar, In Progress, Needs Review, Done, Blocked — gives clients visibility and eliminates anxiety-driven check-in messages.
  • 6
    Document your communication expectations on Day 1: working hours, preferred channel, response time, update schedule, and urgent protocol. Clients who agreed to these boundaries cannot dispute them later.
  • 7
    The first 30 days are about reliability and communication — not volume. Deliver on time, communicate proactively, improve after feedback, and send the Monday update every week.
  • 8
    Use a monthly milestone framework with fixed dates for calendar submission, batch completion, data pull, and report delivery. Put this in the contract as an attachment.
  • 9
    Handle scope creep with the 3-step framework: acknowledge warmly, name the boundary, offer a paid path forward. Never say yes for free — and never refuse without an alternative.
  • 10
    Every project ends with a professional goodbye: final deliverables, handover document, access removal, final invoice, and a testimonial ask with a prompt and a link. The ending is your last impression — and it determines whether you get the referral.
